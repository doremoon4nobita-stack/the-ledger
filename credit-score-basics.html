Credit scores, explained: what actually moves the number

7 min read

A credit score is a three-digit number, usually between 300 and 850, that summarizes how risky you look to a lender. It doesn't measure your income, your savings, or how "good" you are with money in any broad sense — it measures one narrow thing: how reliably you've paid back money you've borrowed in the past.

That narrowness is actually useful once you understand it, because it means the score responds to a small, specific set of behaviors — not to how much you earn or how frugal you are.

What actually makes up the number

The exact formula is proprietary, but the major scoring models weigh roughly the same factors, in roughly this order of importance:

  1. Payment history (about 35%). Have you paid on time? This is the single biggest factor. One missed payment, especially 30+ days late, can drop a score noticeably and stays on your report for years.
  2. Credit utilization (about 30%). This is how much of your available credit you're using. Maxing out a card — even if you pay it off in full every month — can hurt your score, because the snapshot the bureau sees is often your statement balance, not your post-payment balance.
  3. Length of credit history (about 15%). Older accounts help. This is one reason to think twice before closing your oldest credit card, even if you don't use it much.
  4. Credit mix (about 10%). Having a mix of account types (credit card, auto loan, etc.) helps slightly, but it's a minor factor — don't take on debt you don't need just to diversify.
  5. New credit inquiries (about 10%). Applying for several new accounts in a short window signals risk and dings the score temporarily.
Rule of thumb: keep your utilization under 30% of your total credit limit, and ideally under 10% if you're trying to maximize your score before a big application like a mortgage.

Building credit from zero

If you've never had a credit card or loan, you don't have a score yet — and that's a different problem from having a low one. A few common starting points:

  • A secured credit card. You put down a cash deposit (say $200–$500) that becomes your credit limit. Used responsibly, it reports to the bureaus just like a normal card.
  • Becoming an authorized user on a family member's older, well-managed card. Their positive history can start showing up on your report.
  • A credit-builder loan offered by some credit unions, where you "borrow" money that's actually held in a locked savings account until you finish paying it off.

What actually hurts your score

The list is shorter than people expect: missed or late payments, high utilization, collections accounts, bankruptcies, and a flurry of new credit applications in a short time. Checking your own score does not hurt it — that's a "soft" inquiry. Applying for new credit is a "hard" inquiry, and that's the one with a small, temporary impact.

How to check yours for free

You're entitled to a free copy of your credit report from each major bureau once a year — many card issuers and banking apps now also show your score for free as an ongoing feature. Checking regularly costs nothing and helps you catch errors or fraud early, which is one of the most overlooked reasons scores drop unexpectedly.

A good score isn't a goal in itself — it's a tool that gets you better interest rates on things you'd need to borrow for anyway, like a car or a home. Treat it that way, and the habits that build it (paying on time, keeping balances low) fall out naturally from good budgeting in general.
