5 debt traps to avoid in your 20s

6 min read

Nobody signs up for debt they think is a bad idea in the moment. Debt traps work because they're marketed as convenience, flexibility, or a reward — not as a cost. Here are the five that catch the most people early in their financial lives, and how to recognize them before they get expensive.

1. Credit card minimum payments

Paying only the minimum feels responsible — you're paying something, on time, every month. But minimums are calculated to keep you in debt as long as possible. On a typical card balance at 20%+ interest, paying only the minimum can take a decade or more to clear, and you'll pay more in interest than the original purchase cost. If you carry a balance, treat "pay more than the minimum" as the single highest-priority use of extra cash.

2. Buy-now-pay-later plans

Splitting a purchase into four interest-free payments feels harmless, and often is — for one purchase. The trap is stacking several BNPL plans at once across different apps, none of which show up together, until the combined payments quietly outgrow what you budgeted. Missed payments also often carry real fees or interest, despite the "interest-free" framing. Treat BNPL as debt, not as a discount, and track it the same way you'd track a credit card.

3. Payday and cash-advance loans

These are marketed for emergencies but built to be extremely difficult to pay off in one cycle, leading to a rollover into a new loan with new fees. The effective annual interest rate can run into the hundreds of percent. If you're ever facing one, an emergency fund (see our guide on building one) is the actual fix — but if you're in the moment and don't have one, exhaust every other option first, including asking for a payment plan directly from whoever you owe.

4. Auto loans stretched to 72–84 months

A longer loan term lowers the monthly payment, which is exactly what makes it easy to say yes to a more expensive car than you'd otherwise buy. The problem: cars depreciate faster than a long loan pays down, so for years you owe more than the car is worth. If the deal falls apart — job loss, needing to sell — you're stuck paying off a car you no longer have. As a rule of thumb, match the loan term to how long you actually plan to keep the car, and keep total loan length under five years where possible.

5. Co-signing without a plan

Co-signing a loan for a friend or family member feels like a favor with no real cost — until a missed payment shows up on your credit report exactly as if you'd missed it yourself. You're not vouching for someone's character; you're legally responsible for the debt. If you do co-sign, get specific about what happens if a payment is missed, and check the loan status regularly rather than assuming no news is good news.

The common thread: every trap here is marketed around convenience or flexibility, and the real cost shows up later, spread out, and easy to miss month to month.

The general pattern to watch for

Debt traps rarely look like debt in the moment — they look like a small monthly payment, a helpful feature, or a favor to someone you care about. Before taking on any new obligation, ask one question: what does this cost if things go wrong, not just if things go right? That single habit avoids most of the traps on this list.
