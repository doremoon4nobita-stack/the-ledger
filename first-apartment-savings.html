How much to save before you move out

7 min read

The monthly rent number is the one everyone budgets for. It's also the one that causes the fewest surprises. The costs that actually catch first-time renters off guard are the ones that hit all at once, in the first month, before you've even unpacked.

The upfront costs, all at once

  • Security deposit. Typically one month's rent, sometimes more depending on your credit or the landlord's policy.
  • First month's rent (and sometimes last month's rent, paid in advance).
  • Application and background check fees. Usually $30–$75 per applicant, and you may pay this more than once if an application is rejected.
  • Broker's fee, in cities where these are common — this can be a full month's rent or more, paid to whoever helped you find the place.
  • Moving costs. A van rental, movers, or even just gas and pizza for friends who help.
  • Basic furniture and setup. A bed, at minimum. Kitchen basics. This adds up fast even buying used or secondhand.
On a $1,500/month apartment with a deposit and first month's rent, you could realistically need $3,500–$4,500 in cash before you've bought a single fork.

A simple way to estimate your number

  1. Add up deposit + first month's rent (+ last month's, if required in your area).
  2. Add a moving buffer — $200–$800 depending on distance and whether you're hiring movers.
  3. Add a furniture/setup buffer — even $500–$1,000 covers the essentials if you shop secondhand.
  4. Add one month of living expenses as a cushion, separate from your emergency fund, since your first paycheck in the new place might not land for weeks.

The ongoing cost people forget

Rent is rarely the whole monthly number. Budget separately for utilities (electric, gas, water, internet), renters insurance (often $10–$20/month and worth every cent — see below), and any building fees not included in rent. As a starting estimate, add 10–15% on top of the listed rent to get a realistic "true monthly cost."

Renters insurance is not optional

It's easy to skip because it's not legally required in most places and landlords rarely chase it. But it's inexpensive — often under $20 a month — and covers your belongings if there's a fire, theft, or water damage, none of which your landlord's insurance covers. Skipping it to save $15 a month is one of the more common regrets first-time renters mention after something goes wrong.

Before you sign anything

  • Confirm the total move-in cost in writing — deposit, fees, and first month's rent, itemized.
  • Read the lease for what you're responsible for — some leases put appliance repairs, pest control, or minor maintenance on the tenant.
  • Ask when and how the deposit is returned, and photograph the apartment's condition on move-in day, before you unpack.

None of this means moving out has to be scary — it just means the "rent" number you see in a listing is the start of the math, not the whole thing. Budget for the full picture and the actual move goes a lot more smoothly.
