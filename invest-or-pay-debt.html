DEBT & INVESTING

Should you pay off debt or invest first?

7 min read

This question comes up the moment someone has extra money for the first time — a raise, a bonus, or just finally having some breathing room. There's a version of this that people overcomplicate, and a version that's actually pretty simple once you separate it into two questions: what's the interest rate, and what's the risk?

The core comparison

Paying off debt is a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate on that debt. If you have a credit card at 22% interest, paying it off is the same as earning a guaranteed, tax-free 22% return — no investment reliably beats that. Investing, on the other hand, has an expected return (historically averaging roughly 7–10% annually for a diversified stock portfolio over long periods) but no guarantee, and real volatility year to year.

Simple rule: if the debt's interest rate is higher than what you could reasonably expect to earn investing, pay off the debt first. If it's lower, investing is usually the better use of that money.

Where the line usually falls

  • High-interest debt (credit cards, most personal loans, payday loans) — typically 15% to 25%+. Pay this off aggressively before investing beyond any employer 401(k) match. Almost nothing beats this guaranteed return.
  • Moderate-interest debt (some auto loans, older private student loans) — roughly 6% to 10%. This is genuinely a judgment call, and either choice is defensible. Many people split extra money between both.
  • Low-interest debt (some mortgages, some federal student loans) — often 3% to 6%. Investing tends to win here over the long run, though paying down debt still has real value: guaranteed peace of mind and lower monthly obligations.

The exception that comes first: the employer match

Before any of the above, if your employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute enough to get the full match before extra debt payoff — a 50% or 100% instant return on your contribution beats paying off nearly any debt. This is the one case where the math is rarely close.

Why some people invest even with higher-rate debt

The math above assumes a purely financial decision, but a few things can reasonably shift the answer: if the debt has a fixed low payment and no risk of the rate changing, and the person strongly values the long-term growth of starting to invest early (time in the market matters more than most people realize), a partial split can make sense psychologically even if it's not the mathematically optimal choice. There's no rule that says it has to be all one or the other.

A practical order of operations

  1. Get the full employer 401(k) match, if offered — this comes first, before debt payoff.
  2. Build a starter emergency fund ($500–$1,000) so a surprise expense doesn't become new debt.
  3. Pay off high-interest debt (15%+) aggressively.
  4. Build your full emergency fund (three to six months of expenses).
  5. Split extra money between moderate-rate debt and investing, or lean toward whichever helps you sleep better at night.
  6. Invest the rest, typically through tax-advantaged accounts first (401(k), IRA) before a regular brokerage account.

The honest answer to "debt or invest" is almost always "it depends on the rate" — but once you know the rate, the decision usually makes itself.
