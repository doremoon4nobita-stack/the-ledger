How to negotiate a raise (without feeling awkward)

6 min read

Asking for a raise feels loaded because it seems like a confrontation. It works better when you treat it as what it actually is: a normal business conversation about compensation matching contribution, based on evidence, not a personal favor you're asking for.

Timing matters more than people think

The best moments to ask are right after a clear win — you shipped a project, hit a target, took on new responsibility — or during a formal review cycle when raises are already being discussed. The worst moments are right after your company announced layoffs, missed earnings, or a hiring freeze. Reading the room first isn't about losing your nerve; it's about not wasting a good case on bad timing.

Build the case before the conversation

  • Document your impact, ideally with numbers: projects completed, revenue influenced, time or costs saved, responsibilities added since your last raise or since you started.
  • Research market rate for your role, experience level, and location using sites like Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, or industry-specific salary surveys. A specific, well-sourced number is far more persuasive than "I feel underpaid."
  • Note any scope creep. If your responsibilities have grown since you were hired or last leveled up, that's a legitimate, low-drama reason for a raise on its own.

What to actually say

You don't need a dramatic speech. A simple structure works well: state that you'd like to discuss your compensation, summarize your impact concisely, reference the market data, and name a specific number or range. For example: "I'd like to talk about my compensation. Over the past year I've [specific accomplishments]. Based on my research, similar roles are paying [range], and I'd like to discuss bringing my salary to [number]."

Naming a specific number, slightly above what you'd actually accept, gives you room to negotiate down and anchors the conversation higher than a vague ask would.

Handling pushback

If the answer is "not right now" or "budget doesn't allow it," don't treat that as final — ask what would need to be true for a raise to happen, and get a specific timeline. "Can we revisit this in three months, and what would you want to see from me by then?" turns a soft no into a concrete plan, and puts the follow-up on the calendar instead of leaving it open-ended.

If the answer is genuinely no

Ask whether there are other levers — a title change, more flexible hours, extra vacation days, professional development budget, or a path to promotion. Compensation isn't only salary, and sometimes what's actually unavailable is budget for a raise specifically, while other forms of value are still on the table.

A few things that quietly undermine the ask

  • Leading with personal financial need (rent went up, a big expense came up) rather than your contribution. It's understandable, but it's not a business case your manager can act on.
  • Comparing yourself to a specific coworker's pay, which puts your manager in an awkward position they usually can't respond to directly.
  • Asking without any number in mind, which shifts the anchor to whatever they offer first, usually lower than you'd have asked for yourself.

The uncomfortable part of asking for a raise is almost always the anticipation, not the conversation itself. Walking in with a documented case and a specific number turns it from an emotional appeal into a straightforward business discussion — which is a conversation most managers have had many times before, and one they're rarely offended by.
