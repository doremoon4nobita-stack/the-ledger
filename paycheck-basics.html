Reading your paycheck: gross pay, net pay, and where it all goes

6 min read

The first paycheck is often smaller than expected, and the pay stub that comes with it looks like a wall of abbreviations. Once you know what each line means, it stops being confusing — it's really just four categories stacked on top of each other.

Gross pay vs. net pay

Gross pay is your full salary or wages before anything is taken out — this is the number in your offer letter. Net pay (sometimes called "take-home pay") is what actually lands in your bank account, after every deduction below. Budgeting off your gross pay is the single most common mistake people make when they first start working — always build your budget around net pay.

The deductions, category by category

Taxes

Federal income tax, and often state and local income tax, are withheld automatically based on the information you gave your employer (your W-4 in the US). Two other lines usually show up regardless of state: Social Security and Medicare, sometimes labeled together as "FICA." These fund specific federal programs and are a fixed percentage of your pay, not something your employer chooses.

Retirement contributions

If you've opted into a 401(k) or similar plan, your contribution is deducted before you ever see the money. This is worth understanding early: if your employer offers a "match" (they contribute an amount based on what you put in), not contributing at least up to the match is effectively turning down free money.

Health insurance and benefits

Your share of health, dental, or vision insurance premiums is typically deducted here, along with anything you've elected into, like an FSA or HSA (accounts that let you set aside pre-tax money for medical expenses).

Other deductions

This can include things like life insurance premiums, union dues, wage garnishments, or repayment of a signing bonus if you leave before a certain date — worth reading closely if anything unfamiliar shows up.

Quick gut check: gross pay minus taxes minus benefits minus retirement contributions should equal your net pay. If the math on your stub doesn't add up, ask HR or payroll — it happens more often than you'd think.

Why your first paycheck might look extra small

A few common, temporary reasons: your first pay period might be a partial one if you didn't start on day one of the cycle; some deductions like health insurance may not kick in until after a waiting period, then get taken out retroactively; and if you're paid biweekly (every two weeks) rather than semi-monthly, your take-home per check will look different from what a monthly salary divided by two would suggest.

What to actually check on every pay stub

  • Your gross pay matches your agreed salary or hourly rate for the hours worked.
  • Tax withholding looks reasonable — too little withheld can mean an unpleasant tax bill later; too much means you're giving the government an interest-free loan.
  • Retirement contributions match what you elected, especially right after enrolling or changing your contribution percentage.
  • No unfamiliar deductions — payroll errors happen, and they're much easier to fix quickly than months later.

Once you can read a pay stub, budgeting off your actual net pay — the number that matters — stops being a guessing game, and figuring out how much you can actually set aside each month gets a lot more accurate.
