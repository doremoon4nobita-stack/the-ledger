Retirement accounts for beginners: 401(k)s and IRAs explained

8 min read

Retirement feels distant enough in your 20s and 30s that it's easy to put off thinking about entirely. The catch is that the accounts built for it get most of their power from time — money contributed at 25 has decades longer to grow than the same amount contributed at 45. You don't need to understand every detail to get started; you need to understand the basic shape of the two accounts almost everyone encounters first.

What a 401(k) actually is

A 401(k) is a retirement account offered through an employer. You contribute a percentage of your paycheck, often automatically, before taxes are taken out — which lowers your taxable income now. The money grows tax-deferred, meaning you don't pay taxes on gains year to year, and you pay ordinary income tax when you withdraw it in retirement.

The single most important feature to understand is the employer match. Many employers will contribute additional money based on what you put in — for example, matching 50% of your contributions up to 6% of your salary. Not contributing enough to get the full match is leaving free money on the table; it's usually the best "return" available anywhere in your financial plan.

What an IRA is, and how it's different

An IRA (Individual Retirement Account) isn't tied to an employer — you open one yourself through a brokerage. It has lower annual contribution limits than a 401(k), but often more flexibility in what you can invest in. There are two common types:

  • Traditional IRA: contributions may reduce your taxable income now, similar to a 401(k), with taxes paid on withdrawal in retirement.
  • Roth IRA: contributions are made with money you've already paid tax on, but withdrawals in retirement — including all the growth — are tax-free. This tends to favor people earlier in their career, when their income (and tax rate) is often lower than it will be later.
Rough rule of thumb for early-career workers: if your income is relatively low now compared to where it's likely to go, a Roth account (Roth 401(k) or Roth IRA) is often the more favorable choice, since you're paying tax at today's lower rate instead of a future, likely higher one.

A simple order for where to put money

  1. Contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match, if one is offered — this comes before almost anything else.
  2. Consider maxing out an IRA next, since it often offers more investment choices and lower fees than an employer plan.
  3. Go back to your 401(k) and contribute further, up to the annual limit, if you have money left to save.

What "investing it" actually means

Putting money into a 401(k) or IRA doesn't automatically invest it — in many plans, uninvested contributions can sit in a low-return cash or money-market option by default. You typically need to select what it's invested in: a common, low-maintenance starting choice for beginners is a low-cost target-date fund, which automatically adjusts its mix of stocks and bonds to get more conservative as you approach retirement age.

The part that actually matters most

Getting the account type exactly right matters less than simply starting. Because of how compounding works, money contributed in your 20s has an outsized advantage over the same amount contributed later — not because you're smarter about it, but purely because it has more time to grow. Starting with something imperfect today usually beats waiting for the ideal plan.
